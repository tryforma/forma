What a college actually costs your family

The published sticker price is close to meaningless in both directions, and families in high-income, high-cost areas are the ones most often blindsided.

Net price calculators, in junior year

Every American college is legally required to publish one. They take about fifteen minutes with a tax return to hand and are reasonably accurate for straightforward finances.

Run them for every school on the list, together, before the list is fixed — not after the offers arrive. This is the highest-value hour available in the entire process and it is free.

The Bay Area trap

Financial aid formulas read household income and assets without adjusting much for local cost of living. A mortgage that consumes half of a household's take-home pay is not treated the way the family experiences it.

Two engineering salaries in Cupertino frequently produce an expected family contribution the family does not recognise as achievable. That is not an error in the calculation; it is what the formula does. The only useful response is to find out in November of junior year rather than April of senior year.

Home equity is treated differently by the two forms The FAFSA does not count primary-residence equity at all. The CSS Profile, required by many private colleges, does — though institutions cap it differently, often at a multiple of income. For a family whose main asset is a house that has appreciated enormously, the difference between the two forms can be very large.

Two forms, two deadlines

  • FAFSA — federal, free, opens in the autumn for the following academic year. File it even if you expect no need-based aid: some merit awards and most federal loans require it.
  • CSS Profile — administered by the College Board, has a fee, required by many private colleges, and asks for considerably more detail. Its deadlines for early applicants are earlier than the FAFSA's, often in November. Check each school's own date.

Where merit money lives

The most selective private colleges — the Ivies, Stanford, MIT — award need-based aid only. There are no merit scholarships. A family above their aid threshold pays the full price, full stop.

Substantial merit money exists one tier down: at private universities working to raise their profile, at out-of-state publics recruiting strong applicants, and at the CSUs and less-selective UCs. Some universities offer very large packages to National Merit Finalists who name them first choice.

A list built entirely of reaches has no merit money in it anywhere. Families should choose that deliberately, not discover it in April.

Offers are more negotiable than families think

A written appeal to the financial aid office succeeds a meaningful fraction of the time. It is a polite letter, not a confrontation, and it costs an evening.

What works: a specific competing offer attached, or documentation of a change in circumstance — a job loss, a medical expense, a second child entering college. What does not work: asking them to match a school they do not consider a peer, or arguing about merit.

Read the offer correctly

Award letters are not comparable at face value and some are close to deliberately confusing. Separate three things before comparing anything:

  • Grants and scholarships — money that is not repaid.
  • Loans — money that is repaid, listed in a way that often looks like aid.
  • Work-study — a job the student has to find and do.

The number to compare across schools is total cost of attendance minus grants only. Everything else is either debt or labour.

Common questions

Does the FAFSA count home equity?

No — the FAFSA excludes primary-residence equity. The CSS Profile, required by many private colleges, does count it, though schools cap it differently. For families whose main asset is an appreciated home, this difference can be very large.

Should I file the FAFSA if I earn too much for aid?

Yes. Some merit awards require it, and it is the gateway to federal loans regardless of income.

Can you negotiate a financial aid offer?

Yes, more often than families expect. A written appeal attaching a specific competing offer, or documenting a change in circumstance, succeeds a meaningful fraction of the time.

How do I compare financial aid offers?

Compare total cost of attendance minus grants and scholarships only. Loans and work-study are debt and labour, not aid, though letters often present all three together.
