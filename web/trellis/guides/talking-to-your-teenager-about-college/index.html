The conversation to have before senior year

Researchers who study achievement pressure in affluent communities describe the same pattern repeatedly: the students most at risk are not the ones who are failing. They are the ones succeeding at something they did not choose.

The sentence that does the damage

It is rarely "you must go to Berkeley." It is the silence after a 93, and the change in tone when the subject changes.

Teenagers read that second signal far more accurately than adults believe, and they draw a conclusion the parent almost never intends: that the affection is conditional on the outcome. Once that conclusion is in place, every subsequent conversation about applications is happening on top of it.

Three questions worth asking in July

Before senior year begins, once, without a laptop open:

  1. What do you actually want out of the next four years? Not which school. What kind of days.
  2. What are you doing right now only because you think I want it? Expect an uncomfortable pause. The pause is the answer.
  3. What would happen in this house if none of the reach schools worked out?

The third is the important one. Answering it out loud, in advance, in an ordinary voice, removes most of the terror from December — and December is coming either way.

Separate the two roles

A parent can be the project manager — deadlines, forms, fees, the shared calendar, the portal logins — or the emotional support. Doing both is very hard, and when it fails it fails on the support side, because the deadlines are concrete and the support is not.

If there are two parents, split it deliberately and say out loud which is which. If there is one, consider handing the project management to the student and keeping the support role, which is the more valuable of the two and the harder to outsource.

The essay is not editable by you

A parent-edited essay is detectable within two paragraphs. The vocabulary changes and the risk disappears — and the risk was the point. Readers are trying to work out whether they would want this person in a seminar, and a polished, careful, adult-voiced essay answers "no" without meaning to.

The useful contribution is asking a question and then leaving the room. What were you actually thinking when that happened? Why did that bother you so much? And then not touching the draft.

The finding worth saying at dinner

The research on this is more consistent than families expect: where a student goes to college matters far less to their eventual outcomes than what they do once they get there. Selectivity has surprisingly little independent predictive power for students who were admissible to selective schools in the first place.

This is not consolation offered after a bad December. It is the actual finding, and it is worth saying in July, in an ordinary tone, when nothing is at stake — because said in April it sounds like a lie told kindly.

One practical rule Pick one evening a week for college conversation, put it on the calendar, and keep the other six free of it. Families that do this report the single largest improvement in the household, and it costs nothing.

If something is actually wrong

Sleep loss, withdrawal from things they used to like, or talk that frightens you is not a phase to be managed until May. Contact the school counselor. In the US, the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline is available by call or text, at any hour.

Common questions

How much should parents be involved in college applications?

Take the administrative work — deadlines, forms, fees, logins — and stay out of the essays. The role that cannot be outsourced is emotional support, and it is hard to do while also being the enforcer.

Should I edit my child’s college essay?

No. A parent-edited essay is detectable quickly: the vocabulary shifts and the risk disappears. Ask questions about the experience instead, then leave the draft alone.

Does it matter where you go to college?

Far less than families expect. For students admissible to selective schools, research consistently finds what they do at college predicts outcomes better than which one they attended.

What are signs of too much college pressure?

Sleep loss, withdrawal from activities they used to enjoy, and a flat affect around a subject they used to like. Contact the school counselor; in the US, 988 is available by call or text at any hour.
